In short
When designers and video editors swap feedback over chat and shared drives, notes get lost between versions. Aravind A and I designed ReviewIT, a web app where a team uploads videos and photos, comments on the exact moment, and tracks every version and its status in one place.
The result
A tested concept. Two potential users found the layout neat and easy to understand, and could get feedback on time without chasing status updates. Their one request, a light theme, shaped our next steps.

01Context

Feedback Lost Between Versions

The work was fine. The feedback loop around it wasn’t.

As designers, we collaborated with video editors every week. An editor reworks a cut, uploads the new version to a shared drive and sends a link. The designer then has to compare the old and new versions and remember what they asked for last time.

Notes get missed, reviews take longer, and small misunderstandings turn into extra rounds. We wanted the feedback to live on the work itself, not in a chat thread.

02Research

Ten Conversations, One Pattern

We spoke to the people stuck in the loop: designers, video editors, product managers and marketers.

User interviews

We interviewed 10 people, some in person and others over Zoom, WhatsApp and Google Meet, then ran a Google Forms survey that about 25 people answered.

Type
Contextual interviews with ten people, watching how they receive and track feedback today
Duration
About ten minutes per person
Channel
In person, Zoom, WhatsApp and Google Meet

What we asked

  1. How do you receive feedback on your work?
  2. Do you find it difficult to receive feedback from colleagues on your video or photo?
  3. How long does it take to review a photo or video?
  4. How do you track tasks?
  5. Can you find the feedback inside the photo or video?
  6. Are you looking for any special features?
  7. Are you looking for a web-based collaboration tool?
  8. Can you name some web-based collaboration tools?

What the survey said

To see how widespread the problems were, we ran a Google Forms survey: about 25 people responded.

  • Nearly 9 in 10found miscommunication happening around feedback
  • 52%said uploading new versions and managing links is a big issue
  • 76%couldn’t track the status of a task in sync

Starting on paper

Concept sketches came first, the skeleton of the interface, before low-fidelity wireframes. We drew the three screens that mattered most: the project home, a file under review, and the list of review links.

06Solution

One Place for Every Review

Projects at a glance

Each project opens as a grid of folders and files. Every card shows its status, such as in progress, to review or approved, and how many comments it has, so a team sees what needs attention before opening anything.

Feedback pinned to the moment

Open a video and the comments sit beside it. Each one carries a timestamp, and the reviewer’s avatar appears on the timeline at that point. Click it to jump straight to the moment. Replies stay in the thread, and File information shows size, resolution, frame rate, upload status and date.

Everything else, one click away

Every new cut lands under the same file, so nobody digs through drive links to compare. Uploading, sharing, permissions and tracking follow the same simple pattern.

ReviewIT

Upload: a New button for file upload, folder upload, a new folder or a new private folder.File options: share, rename, move, copy, update, duplicate, make private, download or delete, and sort by date, name, type, size, status or comments.Notifications: new comments, tasks added, project assignments and status changes, in one panel.Versions: every cut of a file under one name: switch between V1, V2 and V3 in the header.Status: no status, approved, in progress or to review, set right beside Share.Permissions: allow downloads, comments and approvals, show all versions, and set the link to expire.Photos: the same comments panel as video, with an option to show comments on the image.Review links: every shared link with its creation date, owner, views, active status and URL.Details: the project’s files with their date, owner, views, comments and versions.

07Visual Design

Dark, Calm and Focused

A dark interface lets photos and video stand out. One teal accent marks actions, and each colour comes in 80, 50 and 20 percent tints for states and surfaces.

  • Teal#5EB6AF · primary
  • Dark#404040 · text
  • Grey#303245 · surfaces
  • AaMulish · semi-bold, medium, regular, light
Why Mulish
A minimalist sans serif designed for both display and text, so one typeface carries headings and small labels alike.

08Testing

Two Users, One Clear Request

We tested the prototype with two potential users, who completed a list of tasks. The response was mostly positive, with one clear request.

  • The layout is neat and easy to understand.
  • The app is nicely divided into clear sections.
  • They got feedback on time, without waiting for status updates.
The request
Some people preferred a light theme, so a switch between light and dark went on our list.
The limits
Two users is a small base: enough to catch obvious problems, not to prove the design works. Next, we’d test with five to eight editors and designers on a real review cycle, and measure how long feedback takes to reach the right version.

09Reflect

What We Learned

Research first
In-depth research on video feedback tools made the project more relevant and valuable for users. Running it and shaping the whole flow was the hardest, most satisfying part.
Details add up
Colours, typography and components shape the experience, individually and together.
Next
Add the light theme, and run more research to flesh out the features and structure of the web app.
Feedback shouldn’t live in a chat thread. ReviewIT keeps it on the moment it’s about.
