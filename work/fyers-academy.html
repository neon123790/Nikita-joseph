01Overview

Beginners don’t lack information. Fear takes over.

Most beginner traders don’t fail because they lack information. They fail because fear and emotion take over once real money is involved. We designed FYERS Learn, a single app where people learn a concept through guided learning paths, then practise it in short replays of real past markets. Afterwards, a score shows whether they stuck to their plan or let emotion decide.

Jerry and I took it from research and Figma prototypes to a working Android app we built ourselves with Claude Code, shipping a new build every week. Along the way we created an AI video workflow that turned one recording of our CEO into about ten lessons a week.

  • 4–5months, research to a live Android build
  • 1app for learning and practice, merged from two
  • ~10lessons a week from one CEO recording
  • Weeklybuilds, designed and coded by designers

02The brief

A ₹100 crore question, and no spec.

FYERS set up an Innovation Lab with one goal: find new ideas that could bring the company ₹100 crore in revenue. Our Chief Experience Officer picked four people from the design team, including himself. Jerry and I were two of them. There was no product spec and no roadmap, just an open question and the freedom to answer it.

We explored several ideas, researched each one, estimated what it could earn and pitched them to our CEO. He backed two:

Learn by Doing

A simulator where you practise trading in real past market scenarios.

FYERS Academy

A learning platform for trading and investing, with lessons, puzzles and exams.

They began as separate products. By the end, they had become one.

03The data

Knowing isn’t doing: most traders lose.

Before designing anything, we wanted to understand why new traders struggle. We used three methods: secondary research, competitive analysis and user interviews. The numbers were stark.

Trading education has never been easier to find, yet results haven’t improved. Something between learning and doing wasn’t working.

05Interviews

10–15 traders, remarkably consistent answers.

We interviewed early, intermediate and advanced traders about how they started, where they learned, how they plan their day and what goes wrong.

  • “My friend or relative trades, so I started.”
  • “I don’t know where to start.”
  • “I’m afraid to act on my plan.”
  • “I panicked and exited.”
  • “I just watch the market and decide.”
  • “More capital means more profit.”
  • “There’s no one to guide me.”
  • “Varsity was easy to learn, but not to practise.”
  • “I couldn’t balance it with work.”

06Problem & goal

Designing for the scared beginner.

Advanced traders already had their own systems. The clearest need was with beginners and early-intermediate traders: they juggle several disconnected products, are unsure where to begin, and lose money to emotion and poor planning, not to a lack of information.

Problem statement

Beginner and early-intermediate traders understand concepts in theory but can’t apply them under market pressure. They have no safe, familiar place to practise and no feedback on how they behave while trading.

Our goal

Help beginners enter the market with confidence: learn a concept, practise it safely in a real-market replay, and see clearly how they behave under pressure.

07Design direction

Every insight became a design decision.

  • I don’t know where to start→Learning paths instead of a course catalogue
  • Easy to learn, not to practise→Learn and Practice in one app
  • Panic exits and rash entries→Tilt Meter
  • No plan for the day→A practice plan before every session
  • More capital, more profit→Risk limit with a calculated daily risk %
  • No one to guide me→ECS report with insights
  • Couldn’t balance it with work→5–8 minute missions
  • Afraid to act→Replays of real past markets on the familiar FYERS interface

08Learn by Doing

How do we know what someone meant to do?

Bipin, our CXO, and I set the early look and feel of the simulator. One question shaped most of the work: how do we understand what a user intends, so we can show them whether they actually did it? It took a few attempts.

  1. Attempt 1

    Psychometric questions

    Profile people with a quiz, then suggest scenarios. Clever on paper, but prescriptive: it told users what kind of trader they were instead of letting them find out, and added friction before the chart.

    Dropped
  2. Attempt 2

    Let users make the plan

    Instead of guessing intent, we asked. Before each session the user builds a practice plan, and that plan becomes the benchmark we measure them against.

    Kept

Then two things broke

The trading window confused people

Users didn’t understand window sessions, so we removed that rule.

60 minutes didn’t fit a phone

We kept the 60 bars but squeezed the session into 5–8 minutes, and swapped the clock for bar progress: urgency without a ticking timer.

09Missions

Practice with a story, not a random chart.

Each session is a market mission taken from a real past scenario. The chart, order window and key controls mirror the real FYERS app, so people also get comfortable with the interface they’ll use for real trades.

  1. 1

    Choose a mission

    Open Volatility Breakout Under Test Choppy Session

  2. 2

    Read the brief

    What kind of market to expect, a 5–8 minute heads-up, and related stock news to set the scene.

  3. 3

    Say what you expect

    Study yesterday’s chart and answer two questions: what will the market do, and where would you enter? This records your thinking before the pressure starts.

  4. 4

    Enter the replay

    No pop-ups, hints or interruptions. Just you, the chart and your plan.

10Measuring emotion

The hardest problem: you can’t ask “are you panicking?”

You can’t stop someone mid-trade to ask how they feel. But every emotion shows up as an action, so we designed around what people do: did they set the stop loss they planned? Widen it out of hope, or tighten it out of fear? Stay within their trade limit?

ECS doesn’t measure profit. It measures discipline. We wanted beginners to see that rash decisions and emotional swings affect their results as much as the market does.

12The pivot

Why learn in one app and practise in another?

Learning has two modes: passive (watching, reading, understanding) and active (trying, failing, adjusting). Our users had said it plainly: easy to learn, not to practise. We had built both modes, in two separate products. So we merged them.

A user could watch a lesson on stop losses and test that idea in a real-market replay a few minutes later, without leaving the app.

13The solution

Pick a path, like choosing a branch of engineering.

Most learning platforms open with a long catalogue. For someone who just told you “I don’t know where to start”, that’s overwhelming. So, like choosing a branch of engineering, you pick a field and go deep.

TradingInvestingDerivatives
  1. Path
  2. Course
  3. Module
  4. Lesson

Keeping learning active

  • Puzzles between modules make recall fun.
  • Mini Learn-by-Doing scenarios inside lessons, to try a concept straight away.
  • Assessments anytime, from simple MCQs to scenario questions that test judgment, not memory.
  • FYERS certifications, with planned tie-ups with well-known institutions.

14Figma to live

It looked real in Figma. So we made it real.

Jerry and I aren’t engineers, but we had Claude Code. We connected it to GitHub, opened VS Code and built the app by prompting, one screen and one interaction at a time. With an Android test phone from the company, we settled into a weekly loop:

  1. Build features with Claude Code
  2. Install and test it ourselves
  3. Review what felt wrong
  4. Refine: ideas, animations, micro-interactions
  5. Ship a new build, every week

Because the designers were also the builders, almost nothing was lost between design intent and the product. When we presented to the CEO, we didn’t show a deck or a Figma link. We handed him a working app on a phone.

15Outcome

What we shipped and proved.

  • A live Android product in about 4–5 months, built by a team of four with designers doing the building
  • One unified app combining passive and active learning
  • A new learning model: paths instead of course catalogues
  • A behaviour-based score (ECS and the Tilt Meter) that measures discipline, not just profit
  • An AI content workflow making about 10 lessons a week, later adopted by the Product team for FYERS explainer videos
  • A new way of working: proof that designers can take an idea from research to a working product

Leadership ultimately chose not to take the product to full launch; finishing it needed deeper commitment from Product and Technology. But the work carried on: the content workflow and our way of designing by building are still in use.

16Learnings

What I’ll carry forward.

  1. Merging the products was the most important decision. Neither was as strong alone. Learning and practice belong in one loop.
  2. Dropping ideas was part of the design work. The quiz, the trading window and the 60-minute session were all reasonable, and real behaviour proved them wrong.
  3. You can design for emotion if you measure behaviour. A moved stop loss, a panic exit, a sudden jump in quantity: patterns you can show people.
  4. Designers who build iterate faster. Weekly builds replaced long handoffs, and the gap between design and product almost disappeared.
  5. Innovation needs an owner. A strong prototype still needs Product and Engineering commitment to grow into a full product.

We set out to build a product that could earn ₹100 crore. What we ended up with was a way to help a scared beginner place their first trade with a plan, and keep to it.
